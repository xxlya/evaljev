How it works

What the console decides

The console pulls one thing out of a stream of decisions: the ones your workflow answered on its own that it probably should not have. Here is every rule it uses to pick them.

The five reasons a decision gets flagged

There are only five. Each is a rule you can check by hand.

The answer broke your schema

The probabilities that came back were not over the options you declared — a label you never defined, a missing one, or numbers that do not add up to 1. Whatever your code did with that answer, it did by accident. Needs no ground truth.

A reviewer disagreed

You recorded what actually happened, and it contradicts what was decided. The only flag that is confirmed rather than suspected.

The same input was decided two ways

An identical input, under the identical question, took a different branch somewhere else in the same stream. The decision is sitting on a boundary. Needs no ground truth.

The model was unsure

It put less probability on its answer than your review line — 0.60 by default, and your number to set. The probability itself, not the API’s confidence field, which is a margin over an even split and so means something different for every question depending on how many options it has. Needs no ground truth.

The step had just changed

That decision point started answering differently partway through this window, so its answers are suspect even when they look confident. Needs no ground truth.

A flag only reaches you when it contradicts what your workflow did. If the workflow already sent that request to a person, the flag is counted as evidence your escalation policy is working, not handed to you as a task.

Three rules it holds itself to

A rate is never a single number

One flip in 36 is not 2.8%; it is somewhere between 0.5% and 14%. Every rate carries that range, because deployment decisions get made on the difference.

“Not measured” never reads as “fine”

Too little evidence means nothing was shown, which is not the same as nothing being wrong. Those checks say so, and nothing is claimed from them.

A change has to clear a test

“Answers moved” only appears when the shift is unlikely to be a coincidence of small numbers and big enough to act on. Both, because either alone is wrong: on a tight distribution, a move of 0.01 is statistically real and worth nothing.

Why any of this is needed

One decision, asked two ways that mean the same thing, five times each. Both phrasings are correct to answer yes.

What gets recorded

One decision, as stored. Generic LLM tracing keeps the prompt and the completion; this keeps the full distribution, the question that produced it, four version fields and the outcome — which is what makes every rule above computable later.
